\begin{abstract}
Infrared--visible fusion is often treated as a global preprocessing gain, yet
the same fused image may improve one detector and degrade another. We introduce
Confidence-Shielded Evaluator-Conditioned Fusion (CS-ECF), a label-free policy
that scores visible, consensus-detail fusion (CDF), PIAFusion, and SPGFusion
candidates with the target detector. CS-ECF averages the five highest
confidences of each candidate and selects a fused input only when its score
reaches 1.5 times that of visible; otherwise, visible is retained. The margin
was frozen before testing.

Across fixed-split LLVIP, grouped six-class M3FD, and official DroneVehicle test
subsets, comprising 12 confirmatory checkpoints, nine checkpoint point
estimates improved over visible, although no individual checkpoint contrast
survived Holm correction and the sign test was not significant (\(p=0.073\)).
The dataset-balanced AP50 gain was 0.0133 with a
training-run-by-group 95\% interval of [0.0026, 0.0261]. Against Always CDF, the
balanced difference was 0.0102 [-0.00057, 0.02980], so superiority over this
fixed input was not established. Removing the shield increased the worst AP50
loss from 0.0040 to 0.0584. Two zero-shot evaluators gained 0.021--0.025 AP50
over visible without separating from CDF. A third evaluator, fine-tuned on
LLVIP, lost 0.0108 AP50 relative to visible; its scene-group interval crossed
zero, although shielding improved the unshielded policy by 0.0153. This negative
check shows that confidence arbitration is not universally portable. The
evidence supports bounded, evaluator-dependent risk control, not uniform
improvement, CDF dominance, or low-latency deployment. Serial candidate
generation and four detector evaluations required approximately
\SI{0.71}{s} per image pair.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Infrared--visible imaging combines the spatial detail and color of visible
sensors with thermal evidence that can remain available when illumination is
poor, smoke reduces contrast, or targets have weak visual texture. This
complementarity has motivated pixel-, feature-, and network-based fusion methods
for surveillance, autonomous perception, and thermal imaging systems.
\cite{ma2019survey,mudavath2026review,zhu2017tophat,zhang2020,ma2019fusiongan,li2019densefuse,xu2022u2fusion,li2021rfn,yi2024diffif,yang2025lfdt}
In detection-oriented applications, the value of a fused image is not determined
by visual appeal alone; it is the change in detector outputs under a specified
operating condition.

Current practice often selects one fused representation and evaluates it with
one detector on one benchmark. This design assumes that a globally preferred
fusion result remains preferable across detector architectures and training
seeds. The assumption is fragile because detectors differ in receptive fields,
confidence calibration, training data, and sensitivity to high-frequency
detail \cite{abdar2021uncertainty,gawlikowski2023uncertainty}. Consequently, a
fusion rule that improves one evaluator may reduce the average precision (AP)
of another on the same image.

Per-image selection offers a natural response: run several fusion candidates
and choose the one with the strongest detector response. Yet unconstrained
selection converts small calibration differences into frequent input switches
and can produce severe losses when a spurious high-confidence response is
mistaken for improved detection. The problem is therefore not only to predict
which fused image looks best, but to decide when the evidence is strong enough
to depart from the visible-image safety baseline, a decision related to the
classical reject option in recognition \cite{chow1970reject}.

Recent infrared--visible fusion and detection studies have optimized shared
fusion networks, cross-modal feature fusion, or hierarchical detectors
\cite{zhao2025cmifdf,gao2026coifsd,an2026mrtdetr,zhang2026hffn}.
General-purpose detectors provide the evaluator side of this problem
\cite{redmon2016yolo,zhao2024rtdetr}. These studies
establish the relevance of joint fusion and detection, but they generally train
or optimize a detector-fusion system. They do not establish a label-free
arbitration rule whose switching threshold is fixed independently of the
confirmatory tests and whose behavior is reported at the level of detector
checkpoint, image, and scene or frame group.

We formulate infrared--visible fusion selection as evaluator-conditioned input
arbitration. CS-ECF evaluates four candidates with the target detector, computes
a top-five confidence score, and accepts a fused candidate only when its score
reaches 1.5 times that of the visible input. The threshold is selected on
validation data and frozen before testing. We evaluate the policy on LLVIP,
M3FD, and DroneVehicle with model-, image-, and group-aware uncertainty,
compare it with fixed fusion and an unshielded selector, and report the cost and
failure boundaries. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item an evaluator-conditioned formulation of fusion-input selection that does
  not use test labels;
  \item a confidence shield that reduces catastrophic switching while retaining
  positive dataset-balanced gains;
  \item a three-dataset confirmation that separates image-level uncertainty,
  scene/group uncertainty, and checkpoint variability, including explicit
  evidence that the M3FD gain is concentrated in the people class, the absence
  of a separated advantage over Always CDF, and a fine-tuned-evaluator
  counterexample to universal portability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Fusion representations}

Classical infrared--visible fusion methods combine complementary evidence in a
hand-designed transform or representation. Representative examples use
multi-scale top-hat transforms, non-subsampled contourlet transforms with
compressed sensing, two-scale saliency decomposition, and three-scale framelet
or shear representations \cite{zhu2017tophat,zhang2016nsct,zhang2021twoscale,wei2017threescale}.
Sparse-representation methods instead describe local image content with learned
or overcomplete dictionaries before applying a coefficient-level fusion rule
\cite{yin2013sparsefusion,yin2015multiscale}. These approaches make target
contrast, edges, and local saliency explicit. Their combining rules are
typically designed for image-level visual quality, however, rather than for the
ranking that a particular detector assigns to the resulting image.

Learned fusion models replace handcrafted combination rules with trainable
feature extraction and reconstruction. DenseFuse, IFCNN, U2Fusion, and
RFN-Nest are representative encoder--decoder or residual designs
\cite{li2019densefuse,zhang2020,xu2022u2fusion,li2021rfn}. Adversarial
formulations add discriminative losses for texture and target preservation
\cite{ma2019fusiongan,hou2021ssgan,yi2021dfpgan,song2022tdgan}. Dual-path
wavelet networks and transformer-based designs subsequently added multi-scale
context and long-range interaction \cite{liu2023dualpath,hao2022vdfefuse,yang2023dglt,an2024mras},
while diffusion models introduced generative priors for fusion
\cite{yi2024diffif,yang2025lfdt}. These methods enlarge the candidate design
space, but they generally define a single fused output. They do not specify how
to choose among several available candidates for a fixed deployed detector.

\subsection{Task-aware fusion and detection}

Task-aware fusion introduces an illumination, semantic, or saliency signal into
the transfer rule. PIAFusion conditions fusion on illumination
\cite{tang2022}; SPGFusion and MTFusion incorporate semantic priors
\cite{zhang2026,zhou2026mtfusion}; and classification-saliency or masked
autoencoder designs use auxiliary representations to decide which features to
retain \cite{xu2021classsaliency,wang2025smae}. Such priors make the transfer
more selective, but a proxy such as illumination or segmentation does not
guarantee a better confidence ordering for every detector.

On the detection side, one-stage and DETR-style architectures provide different
confidence behaviour and receptive-field structure \cite{redmon2016yolo,wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10,zhao2024rtdetr}.
Recent infrared--visible systems integrate fusion with detection through shared
backbones, cross-modal feature exchange, saliency masking, or hierarchical
fusion \cite{zhao2025cmifdf,gao2026coifsd,an2026mrtdetr,zhang2026hffn,xiang2025unifusod,yue2022lowlight}.
Joint optimization can align the fused representation with the detector, but
the resulting coupling is learned for a specified training objective. It does
not by itself provide a label-free rule for selecting among already generated
fusion candidates when the target checkpoint or data composition changes.

\subsection{Evaluation and selection boundary}

Fusion quality has commonly been assessed with reference or no-reference image
metrics \cite{ma2019survey,mudavath2026review}. Those scores summarise source
retention or perceptual quality; they do not identify which of several inputs
will produce the higher AP for a particular detector. Multispectral
detection studies show that thermal evidence can improve robustness under
poor illumination \cite{li2010thermal,guo2024prior}, but gains depend on
architecture, training data, and the operating regime
\cite{abdar2021uncertainty,gawlikowski2023uncertainty}. The same dependence is
visible across the detector checkpoints used in this study.

The decision to depart from a visible-image fallback resembles the classical
reject option \cite{chow1970reject}, but the object of the decision is an input
candidate rather than a class prediction. The threshold must therefore be
specified separately from accuracy, fixed before confirmation, and evaluated
against both average gain and worst-case loss. Reporting only a fused-image
score or a single benchmark pairing leaves that decision boundary untested.

\subsection{Positioning}

CS-ECF does not introduce another image-fusion operator. It treats an existing
fusion library as a set of alternative inputs and uses the target detector as a
label-free evaluator. Relative to task-aware networks, it requires neither a
new fusion model nor joint detector-fusion training. Relative to fixed
image-quality rankings, it measures the quantity of interest directly and
separates detector checkpoints, scene or frame groups, and independent
evaluators. The contribution is therefore an arbitration policy plus a
risk-boundary evaluation, not a claim that any one fused representation is
universally superior.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation}

Let \(x_v\) and \(x_i\) denote a registered visible--infrared pair, and let
\(\mathcal{D}\) denote the target detector checkpoint. A candidate
\(c\in\mathcal{C}\) produces an input image \(x_c\), where
\(\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{vis},\mathrm{cdf},\mathrm{pia},\mathrm{spg}\}\).
The policy returns one candidate per pair without access to ground-truth boxes:
\begin{equation}
g(x_v,x_i;\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{C}.
\end{equation}
The final detection output is
\(\mathcal{D}(x_{g(x_v,x_i;\mathcal{D})})\). The detector is therefore reused
for candidate evaluation and final inference; it is not an external label
oracle.

\subsection{Fusion candidates}

The visible input is always included as a safety candidate. CDF combines a
pixel-mean image and an IFCNN image through sign-consistent high-frequency
details. Let \(x_m\) denote the pixel-mean image, let \(x_f\) denote the IFCNN
image, and let \(G_\sigma\) denote Gaussian blur with \(\sigma=5\). The
corresponding detail images are
\(d_m=x_m-G_\sigma*x_m\) and \(d_i=x_f-G_\sigma*x_f\). Their consensus detail
is
\begin{equation}
d(x)=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{sign}(d_m+d_i)\min(|d_m|,|d_i|), & d_m d_i>0,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A thermal-validity mask \(M\) is formed from the infrared top-hat response,
infrared intensity, visible luminance, connected components, and smoothing.
The top-hat threshold \(q_{\mathrm{TH}}=0.95\) is the response quantile above
which a pixel is considered thermally salient; it is distinct from the
candidate score \(q_c\) defined below. We used a \(31\times31\) elliptical
top-hat kernel, an infrared floor of 120, visible luminance below its 75th
percentile, \(5\times5\) morphological opening, and retention of connected
components with at least 64 pixels. The surviving mask was dilated and
smoothed with \(31\times31\) kernels.
The shared detail is injected into the LAB luminance channel:
\begin{equation}
L' = L + \alpha M d.
\end{equation}
The frozen settings are \(q_{\mathrm{TH}}=0.95\), \(\alpha=0.20\), and
\(\sigma=5\).
PIAFusion and SPGFusion are generated with their official model definitions and
pretrained weights under the same image-pair protocol.
\cite{zhang2020,tang2022,zhang2026}

\subsection{Confidence-shielded arbitration}

For candidate \(c\), let
\(s_{c,1}\ge s_{c,2}\ge\cdots\) be detector confidences. We define
\begin{equation}
q_c=\frac{1}{\min(K,n_c)}
\sum_{j=1}^{\min(K,n_c)}s_{c,j},
\qquad K=5,
\end{equation}
where \(n_c\) is the number of detections. Candidates with no detections receive
\(q_c=-1\). The highest-scoring candidate is
\(c^\ast=\arg\max_c q_c\), with ties resolved in favor of visible. The selected
input is
\begin{equation}
c=
\begin{cases}
c^\ast, & c^\ast\ne\mathrm{vis}\ \text{and}\ q_{c^\ast}\ge1.5q_{\mathrm{vis}},\\
\mathrm{vis}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The four-candidate library and the top-five score were fixed before the
confirmatory tests. The score averages five boxes rather than relying on one
maximum, while retaining a small set that is not dominated by the low-confidence
tail. The candidate library was chosen to span visible, region-selected,
learned, and published fusion families. We evaluated margins
\(\{1.00,1.10,1.20,1.30,1.50,1.75,2.00\}\) on the 150 LLVIP validation images
and 400 M3FD validation images using two detectors per dataset, before scoring
the confirmatory test images. The objective was to maximize the
dataset-balanced validation gain subject to no evaluator losing more than
\(0.0005\) AP50. Within this fixed top-five rule, \(1.5\) was the first setting
satisfying the constraint and gave the largest balanced validation gain. It
switched \(4.7\%\) of validation decisions, compared with \(11.4\%\) at 1.2.
Table~\ref{tab:score-margin} reports a post-freeze sensitivity check over other
score depths and margins. The expanded grid was not used to reselect the policy
after test performance was observed. The complete pre-freeze search is reported
in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Datasets and evaluators}

The official LLVIP partitions are larger than the experiment subsets
\cite{jia2021}. The 300-image test subset contains the first 300 pairs after a
uniformly random shuffle (seed 0) of official test pairs with non-empty labels.
The release used here contains 3,463 test pairs, all with non-empty labels;
3,163 eligible pairs were therefore not evaluated.
The 1,500-image training subset comprises the lexicographically first 1,500
official training pairs with non-empty labels. Within this ordered subset, the
first 1,350 pairs form training and the remaining 150 form validation.
Train/test, validation/test and train/validation stem intersections are all
empty; the three partitions contain 4,479, 419 and 744 pedestrian boxes. The
eight LLVIP confirmatory checkpoints are YOLO11n trained on visible images, a
modality-agnostic YOLO11n control, YOLO26n seeds 0--2, RT-DETR-l, and YOLOv8n
seeds 0 and 1. YOLO11n seed 0 is used for each YOLO11n family; four
independently trained runs of each family are retained as a seed sensitivity
analysis. All best checkpoints were selected using the 150 validation images,
not the scored test subset.

M3FD contributes a grouped six-class split of 1,600 training, 400 validation
and 200 test images \cite{liu2022}. Frames are partitioned by consecutive
100-frame group using a fixed group seed, so adjacent frames do not cross
splits. The two frame groups in the test set are specified in the released
manifest. One YOLO26n and one YOLOv8n visible-trained six-class evaluator were
selected on the validation groups.

DroneVehicle contributes 300 image pairs from three fixed consecutive
100-frame groups of the official test archive \cite{sun2022}. The groups were
selected with a predefined seed of 2037 before evaluation. The YOLO11x-OBB
best and last checkpoints come from one 150-epoch training run (batch size 32,
640-pixel input, seed 0). Because these checkpoints share training data, they
are treated as one model cluster rather than independent architectures. The
source archive has 4,430,343,899 bytes and SHA-256
\texttt{\seqsplit{94BF47E493E7A57FD5D900439F614F339E7B6C7181E4D781BCFEBAEB963FFD8E}}
to identify the exact release used for the test groups.

\paragraph{External evaluator stress tests}
We also applied the frozen selector to three checkpoints that were not used to
construct the policy or select its margin. YOLOv9t and YOLOv10s were
COCO-pretrained models without LLVIP fine-tuning, and only their person-class
detections were retained \cite{wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10}. A third
YOLOv9t was initialized from the same COCO weights and fine-tuned for a
pre-specified 40 epochs on the 1,350 LLVIP visible training images. Training
used AdamW, a learning rate of 0.002 with three-epoch warm-up and cosine decay,
weight decay \(5\times10^{-4}\), batch size 16, 640-pixel inputs, horizontal
flipping, and brightness/contrast jitter. A reproducible PIL-based loader and
the Ultralytics detection loss were used. The last checkpoint was fixed after
epoch 40; no epoch was selected using test results.

\paragraph{Detection evaluation}
Predictions use a 640-pixel inference size and a confidence floor of
\(0.001\). For YOLO models, non-maximum suppression uses an IoU threshold of
\(0.7\). At each class and each IoU threshold from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of
0.05, predicted boxes are assigned to ground-truth boxes by one-to-one
maximum-cardinality matching over valid pairs. The resulting true-positive
flags and confidence scores are pooled across images within a checkpoint and
integrated over the 101-point interpolated precision-recall curve using
Ultralytics 8.4.189 \cite{jocher2026ultralytics}. AP50 is the mean over the
classes present in the dataset; AP50--95 further averages the ten IoU
thresholds.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Point estimates use pooled AP50 after concatenating detections across test
images within a checkpoint. Image bootstrap intervals resample images within a
checkpoint. Group bootstrap intervals resample scenes or consecutive frame
groups and are the primary clustered intervals. A detector checkpoint is the
unit for architecture-level summaries; images and scenes/frame groups are
repeated observations within a checkpoint and are never treated as independent
training runs. Checkpoints from one training run remain one run cluster.
The dataset-balanced estimand gives LLVIP, M3FD, and DroneVehicle equal weight.
M3FD contains only two test groups and LLVIP seven scenes, so its group
intervals are explicitly finite-cluster summaries.

The confirmatory multiplicity family contains the 12 prespecified
CS-ECF-minus-visible checkpoint contrasts. We used 2,000 paired
scene/frame-group bootstrap resamples per checkpoint, computed two-sided
bootstrap p-values, and applied Holm correction at family-wise
\(\alpha=0.05\); Benjamini--Hochberg q values are also reported. The number of
positive checkpoints is additionally summarised with an exact sign test and is
not treated as a substitute for effect sizes. The dataset-balanced estimate
uses 10,000 run-cluster-by-group resamples; 20 independent 5,000-draw reruns
were used to check interval stability. The CS-ECF-versus-Always CDF
dataset-balanced contrast is one prespecified secondary estimand; its
per-dataset values are descriptive rather than a separate confirmatory family.
The three external-evaluator contrasts are an exploratory stress-test family,
with Benjamini--Hochberg correction across those three checks.

We define detection-level ECE using ten fixed confidence bins and also report
the Brier score for the external evaluators. These calibration quantities are
descriptive summaries of matched detections, not calibrated class
probabilities. Confidence selection scores use the top-five mean before any
calibration adjustment.

The training-run-by-group bootstrap used the parameters above with
\texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng(2219)} in Python 3.10.22 and NumPy 2.2.6.
Spearman association tests used SciPy 1.15.3.
The Supplementary Material provides the complete split counts, all
per-checkpoint CS-ECF and Always CDF results, the validation-policy record, and
the multiplicity, baseline, class, calibration, and thermal analyses. The
public analysis script reconstructs each checkpoint
and rejects it if its AP does not agree with the published aggregate table
before computing the paired interval.

\subsection{Implementation and cost}

At a standardized fusion input of \(384\times512\), FP32 and batch size one on
an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti, the CDF pipeline required \SI{22.3}{ms}, PIAFusion
\SI{37.7}{ms}, and SPGFusion \SI{516.3}{ms}. Four serial YOLO11x-OBB evaluations
at an input size of 640 required \SI{130.0}{ms}, giving an estimated serial
total of \SI{706.3}{ms}. This estimate excludes model loading or swapping, data
loading, and final detection post-processing. SPGFusion's additional peak
activation memory was \SI{1453.5}{MiB}; per-stage memory values must not be
summed as an end-to-end peak.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Fusion utility is evaluator-dependent}

Across the 12 confirmatory checkpoints, the same candidate library produced
both positive and negative gains (Fig.~\ref{fig:evaluator}). CS-ECF improved
nine checkpoint point estimates. Its dataset-balanced AP50 gain was 0.0133,
with a training-run-by-group hierarchical 95\% interval of [0.0026, 0.0261].
The exact sign test for 9/12 positive contrasts gave \(p=0.073\). After Holm
correction across the 12 checkpoint family, no individual contrast had
adjusted \(p<0.05\); the smallest raw p-value, for LLVIP/YOLO11n, was 0.005
(Holm-adjusted \(p=0.060\)). We therefore interpret the balanced estimate as a
small average effect and do not claim that any individual checkpoint improved
significantly. The unshielded
selector improved seven of 12 checkpoints, had a larger dataset-balanced mean of
0.0332, and produced a worst loss of 0.0584.

\subsection{Dataset-specific behavior}

On LLVIP, the mean gain was 0.0016 and five of eight checkpoints improved; the
model and image intervals crossed zero. On M3FD, both six-class checkpoints
improved, with a mean gain of 0.0111. On the independent DroneVehicle test
subset, the best and last OBB checkpoints improved by 0.0251 and 0.0293; the
combined group interval was [0.0002, 0.0406]. All three DroneVehicle groups were
non-negative, but one gained only 0.0002. The two OBB checkpoints originated
from the same training run and are not independent architectures.

\subsection{Controls and headroom}

We added label-free controls and a labelled upper bound to determine whether
the confidence signal adds information beyond choosing a visually strong
candidate. The VIF selector chose the fused candidate with the highest mean
reference VIF to visible and infrared, and the SSIM selector used the analogous
mean structural similarity. Both selected among the three fused candidates
only, whereas the entropy selector chose among all four inputs. All image
statistics were computed on \(256\times256\) grayscale inputs. Uniform random
selection sampled the four candidates independently for each image and
checkpoint over 500 allocations. The oracle selected the candidate with the
highest per-image AP50 using test labels and is an upper bound only. Images
without ground-truth boxes received an AP50 of zero for every candidate, so
ties resolved to visible.

The common \(256\times256\) grayscale representation made the image-quality
inputs comparable across source resolutions and detector architectures. These
selectors used no target labels.

The maximum-reference-VIF selector produced a dataset-balanced gain of 0.0008
with 8/12 positive checkpoints, far below the confidence-based policy.
SSIM and entropy selection were strongly negative. CS-ECF lies about 40\% of
the distance from the mean uniform-random policy to the per-image oracle on
dataset-balanced AP50, showing both that detector confidence adds value beyond
image-only statistics and that substantial headroom remains.

\subsection{The shield is a risk-control mechanism}

Without the shield, the policy selected fused candidates on most LLVIP and
DroneVehicle images and occasionally produced large confidence gains that did
not translate into AP. The 1.5 margin reduced switches and lowered the worst
AP50 loss from 0.0584 to 0.0040. On DroneVehicle, however, the shield also
reduced the mean gain from 0.1040 to 0.0272. This trade-off is the central
quantitative meaning of risk control in this study.

\subsection{Fixed baselines and class concentration}

Always CDF improved 10 of 12 checkpoints and had a dataset-balanced gain of
0.0031, but remained negative on both M3FD detectors. Always PIAFusion and
Always SPGFusion had negative dataset-balanced gains because their strong
DroneVehicle performance did not transfer to the other datasets. In the M3FD
six-class evaluation, the CS-ECF gain was concentrated in people:
YOLO26n improved by 0.0214 and YOLOv8n by 0.1090, while the other five classes
changed by at most 0.0030 in absolute AP50 (Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal}). The people
class contains 166 of 2,022 test annotations (8.2\%), but AP50 weights classes
equally. It accounts for 87.7\% and 99.6\% of the positive class-mean gain for
the two detectors, respectively. Image-bootstrap intervals were
\([-0.0070,+0.0498]\) for YOLO26n and \([+0.0440,+0.1913]\) for YOLOv8n; only
the latter excludes zero. With just two test frame groups, these class
intervals are descriptive rather than stable group-level inference. The
evidence therefore supports a pedestrian/people-focused interpretation, not
uniform six-class improvement.

\subsection{Thermal feature association and mask ablation}

On LLVIP, infrared top-hat contrast showed a weak positive association with
switching (Spearman \(\rho=0.151\), \(p=0.009\)), whereas visible luminance was
only weakly negative (\(\rho=-0.104\), \(p=0.071\)). On M3FD, thermal contrast
showed a moderate positive association (\(\rho=0.320\), \(p<10^{-5}\)).
On DroneVehicle, however, both thermal contrast (\(\rho=-0.403\)) and visible
luminance (\(\rho=-0.441\)) were negatively associated with switching. A
high-thermal/low-visible median split did not produce a consistently larger AP
gain than the remaining images.

A fixed-input mask ablation gave a smaller but consistent thermal effect on
DroneVehicle. Replacing the thermal-validity mask with global detail injection
changed the CDF gain from 0.0060 to 0.0057 for the best checkpoint and from
0.0050 to 0.0038 for the last checkpoint. The thermal mask added 0.0007 AP50 on
average relative to global injection.

\subsection{Registration sensitivity}

We estimated translation residuals for all 300 LLVIP test pairs with ECC on
normalized Sobel edges. The median residual was 2.53 pixels. The median
test-set person box was 109 pixels wide and 246 pixels high, so the median
residual corresponded to 2.3\% of median box width and 1.0\% of median box
height. On the 75 best-aligned pairs (residual at most 1.61 pixels), the mean
CS-ECF gain across the eight LLVIP checkpoints was 0.0007, with five positives.
On the 75 worst-aligned pairs, the mean gain was 0.0020, with six positives. Most
checkpoint-specific intervals still crossed zero, so these subsets do not
identify a separate registration regime. They do show that the observed gain
is not confined to the best-aligned quartile. The same residual audit was not
available for M3FD or DroneVehicle.

\section{Discussion}

The central finding is that fusion utility is not an intrinsic property of a
fused image alone. A candidate can improve one detector's confidence ordering
while harming another's, and a policy that ignores this dependence can select
inputs that appear confident but do not improve AP. CS-ECF responds by using
the target detector as an input arbiter and requiring a large visible-relative
margin before switching.

The shield's value is clearest in the tail. The unshielded selector achieved a
higher dataset-balanced average on DroneVehicle, where both PIAFusion and
SPGFusion were strong, but it also produced severe LLVIP losses. The 1.5 margin
deliberately favors visible fallback under ambiguous evidence. This is
consistent with a deployment rule in which a small probability of a large
regression is more costly than a missed opportunity for a moderate gain.

The comparison with Always CDF further bounds the use case. When CDF is known
to be stable for a fixed evaluator family, the simpler fixed input remains
preferable. CS-ECF is relevant when the deployed detector or data domain can
move the best fusion family, as observed between pedestrian, mixed-class and
vehicle-focused evaluation. Its small balanced advantage should therefore not
be read as a demonstrated replacement for CDF everywhere: the interval
included zero.

The multiplicity analysis limits the language further. Although the
dataset-balanced interval is positive, no individual checkpoint contrast
survived Holm correction, and the 9/12 sign test was not significant. The
appropriate claim is a small average effect under a prespecified composite
estimand, not a collection of proven checkpoint-level wins.

The external evaluators provide a more heterogeneous picture. The two zero-shot
checkpoints support a visible-relative gain but not superiority over Always CDF.
The LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t provides the opposite result: CS-ECF falls below
visible, while shielding still improves on the unshielded selector. Its
detection-level calibration error was about five times larger than those of
the zero-shot evaluators, and 41.4\% of the images it switched to fusion
degraded in per-image AP50. This is consistent with a confidence-calibration
failure, but the domain-trained checkpoint also has lower test performance and
a larger train-to-test shift. The current design cannot separate those
explanations causally.

Four boundaries constrain the interpretation. First, the strongest M3FD effect
is class-specific: people detection improves substantially while the remaining
classes are nearly unchanged. Second, the DroneVehicle confirmation uses two
checkpoints from the same training run; the checkpoints share training data and
are correlated. Third, the domain-fine-tuned YOLOv9t demonstrates that an
unseen target detector can reverse the sign of the visible-relative contrast.
The policy should therefore be validated for each deployed evaluator rather
than assumed portable.

A fourth boundary concerns the LLVIP training subset. It was selected
lexicographically rather than by scene-stratified sampling, so its scene
composition may differ from the full official training partition. The test
subset was sampled randomly from all 3,463 non-empty official test pairs.

The thermal stratification adds another limitation. Thermal contrast may
increase the likelihood of selecting fused evidence in LLVIP and M3FD, but the
association reverses on DroneVehicle. Sensor-level thermal evidence and
detector confidence therefore capture different information. Neither should be
interpreted as a universal utility score. The appropriate claim is thermal
fusion utility under a detector-conditioned decision rule, not thermal dominance.

The cost analysis also matters. SPGFusion dominates latency, and four detector
evaluations are not free. CS-ECF is best viewed as a risk-controlled system for
settings in which the target detector is already run and the cost of a
catastrophic input choice is high. It is not a low-latency replacement for a
single fusion operator. Candidate pruning and early termination were not
implemented or claimed; measuring their latency--accuracy trade-off is a
separate deployment study.

A practical decision sequence follows from these boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item use Always CDF when the detector is fixed and validation shows stable
  CDF behaviour;
  \item consider CS-ECF when the evaluator or domain may change and the target
  model can be calibrated and revalidated on held-out groups;
  \item use the shield when a large regression is more costly than a missed
  moderate gain;
  \item reject the policy for latency-sensitive deployment unless candidate
  pruning or early termination is evaluated separately;
  \item do not deploy the policy after a fine-tuning jump that substantially
  worsens confidence calibration without rerunning threshold validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

We showed that infrared--visible fusion utility is evaluator-conditioned and
proposed CS-ECF, a confidence-shielded input-arbitration policy that falls back
to visible unless a detector-supported fusion candidate clears a fixed
visible-relative margin. Across three test datasets and 12 checkpoints, the
policy improved nine checkpoint point estimates but no individual contrast
survived Holm correction; the prespecified dataset-balanced AP50 gain was
0.0133, and the worst observed degradation was reduced from 0.0584 to 0.0040.
The dataset-balanced gain over the stronger Always CDF baseline was 0.0102,
with a run-cluster by group interval that included zero. The
two unused zero-shot evaluators also improved over visible by 0.021--0.025 AP50,
without separating from Always CDF. An additional LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t
exposed a counterexample: CS-ECF lost 0.0108 AP50 relative to visible, although
it improved the unshielded selector by 0.0153. The evidence supports bounded,
evaluator-dependent risk control for pedestrian-centric and mixed
infrared--visible detection. It does not support uniform six-class improvement,
universal portability across evaluators, independent replication of the
DroneVehicle result from one training run, or dominance over Always CDF. The
additional cost of SPGFusion and four detector evaluations must also be
considered in deployment.
