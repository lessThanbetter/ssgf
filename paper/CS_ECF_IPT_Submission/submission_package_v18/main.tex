\begin{abstract}
Infrared and visible fusion is often treated as a universally beneficial
preprocessing step, yet one fused image can improve one detector and degrade
another. We introduce
Confidence-Shielded, Evaluator-Conditioned Fusion (CS-ECF), a label-free policy
that scores visible, consensus-detail fusion (CDF), PIAFusion, and SPGFusion
candidates with the target detector. Each candidate is scored by the mean of
up to five highest confidences. CS-ECF uses a fused candidate only when its score
reaches 1.5 times that of visible; otherwise, it retains visible. This margin
was fixed before confirmatory testing.

We evaluated CS-ECF on a fixed-split subset of LLVIP, a grouped six-class split
of M3FD, and an official-test subset of DroneVehicle, using 12 confirmatory
checkpoints. Nine of the 12 checkpoint contrasts were positive, but none
survived Holm correction and the exact sign test was not significant
(\(p=0.073\)). The dataset-balanced AP50 gain was 0.0133, with a 95\%
training-run-by-group interval of [0.0026, 0.0261]. Against Always CDF, the
balanced difference was \(+0.0102\), but its interval
\([-0.00057, 0.02980]\) included zero, so superiority was not established.
Removing the shield increased the magnitude of the worst AP50 loss from 0.0040
to 0.0584. Two zero-shot evaluators improved AP50 over visible by
0.021--0.025, but neither separated from Always CDF. An
LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t lost 0.0108 AP50 relative to visible; shielding
nevertheless improved the no-shield selector by 0.0153. CS-ECF did
not transfer uniformly across evaluators. The
evidence supports bounded, evaluator-dependent risk control, not uniform
improvement, CDF dominance, or low-latency deployment. Candidate generation and
four detector passes took approximately \SI{0.71}{s} per image pair.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Visible and infrared imaging provide complementary evidence. Visible cameras
capture fine spatial detail and colour, whereas infrared sensors can retain
target contrast under poor illumination or smoke. These properties have
motivated pixel-, feature-, and network-based fusion methods for surveillance,
autonomous perception, and thermal imaging systems
\cite{ma2019survey,mudavath2026review,zhu2017tophat,zhang2020,ma2019fusiongan,li2019densefuse,xu2022u2fusion,li2021rfn,yi2024diffif,yang2025lfdt}.
For object detection, however, the value of a fused image depends on more than
visual appeal. It is determined by the change in detector outputs under a
specified operating condition.

Current studies commonly select one fused representation and evaluate it with
one detector on one benchmark. This design assumes that a globally preferred
fusion result remains preferable across detector architectures and training
seeds. The assumption is fragile because detectors differ in receptive-field
size, confidence calibration, training data, and sensitivity to high-frequency
detail \cite{abdar2021uncertainty,gawlikowski2023uncertainty}. A fusion rule
that improves one evaluator may therefore reduce the average precision (AP) of
another on the same image.

Per-image selection offers a natural response: evaluate several fusion
candidates and choose the one with the strongest detector response.
Unconstrained selection, however, can turn small calibration differences into
frequent input switches and severe AP losses when a spurious high-confidence
response is mistaken for better detection. The task is therefore to decide
when the evidence is strong enough to depart from the visible-image fallback,
which is related to the classical reject option in recognition
\cite{chow1970reject}.

Recent infrared--visible fusion and detection studies have optimized shared
fusion networks, cross-modal feature fusion, or hierarchical detectors
\cite{zhao2025cmifdf,gao2026coifsd,an2026mrtdetr,zhang2026hffn}.
General-purpose detectors provide the evaluator side of this problem
\cite{redmon2016yolo,zhao2024rtdetr}. These studies show why fusion and
detection should be considered jointly, but they generally train or optimize a
detector--fusion system. They do not provide a label-free arbitration rule with
a threshold fixed independently of confirmatory testing and evaluated at the
checkpoint, image, and scene or frame-group levels.

We formulate infrared--visible fusion selection as evaluator-conditioned input
arbitration. CS-ECF evaluates four candidates with the target detector,
computes a top-five confidence score, and accepts a fused candidate only when
its score reaches 1.5 times that of the visible input. The threshold is selected
on validation data and frozen before testing. We evaluate the policy on LLVIP,
M3FD, and DroneVehicle using checkpoint-, image-, and group-aware uncertainty. We
also compare it with fixed fusion and a no-shield selector, and we report its
cost and failure boundaries. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a label-free, evaluator-conditioned formulation of fusion-input
  selection;
  \item a confidence shield that reduces catastrophic switching while
  preserving positive dataset-balanced gains;
  \item a three-dataset risk-boundary evaluation that separates image, group,
  and checkpoint uncertainty and reports the people-class concentration, the
  absence of a separated advantage over Always CDF, and a fine-tuned-evaluator
  counterexample to universal portability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Fusion representations}

Classical infrared--visible fusion methods combine complementary evidence using
handcrafted transforms or representations. Representative examples include
multi-scale top-hat transforms, non-subsampled contourlet transforms with
compressed sensing, two-scale saliency decomposition, and three-scale framelet
or shear representations \cite{zhu2017tophat,zhang2016nsct,zhang2021twoscale,wei2017threescale}.
Sparse-representation methods instead describe local image content with learned
or overcomplete dictionaries before applying a coefficient-level fusion rule
\cite{yin2013sparsefusion,yin2015multiscale}. These methods explicitly preserve
target contrast, edges, and local saliency. Their fusion rules are typically
designed for image-level visual quality rather than for the ranking assigned by
a particular detector.

Learned fusion models replace handcrafted combination rules with trainable
feature extraction and reconstruction. DenseFuse, IFCNN, U2Fusion, and RFN-Nest
are representative encoder--decoder or residual designs
\cite{li2019densefuse,zhang2020,xu2022u2fusion,li2021rfn}. Adversarial
formulations add discriminative losses for texture and target preservation
\cite{ma2019fusiongan,hou2021ssgan,yi2021dfpgan,song2022tdgan}.
Wavelet-based dual-path networks and transformer-based designs have since added
multi-scale context and long-range interaction
\cite{liu2023dualpath,hao2022vdfefuse,yang2023dglt,an2024mras}, while diffusion
models introduced generative priors for fusion \cite{yi2024diffif,yang2025lfdt}.
These methods broaden the candidate design space but generally produce a single
fused output. They do not specify how to select among several available
candidates for a fixed deployed detector.

\subsection{Task-aware fusion and detection}

Task-aware fusion introduces an illumination, semantic, or saliency signal into
the transfer rule. PIAFusion conditions fusion on illumination
\cite{tang2022}; SPGFusion and MTFusion incorporate semantic priors
\cite{zhang2026,zhou2026mtfusion}; and classification-saliency or masked
autoencoder designs use auxiliary representations to decide which features to
retain \cite{xu2021classsaliency,wang2025smae}. Such priors make the transfer
more selective, but a proxy such as illumination or segmentation does not
guarantee a better confidence ordering for every detector.

One-stage and DETR-style detectors exhibit different confidence behaviour and
receptive-field structure \cite{redmon2016yolo,wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10,zhao2024rtdetr}.
Recent infrared--visible systems have integrated fusion with detection through shared
backbones, cross-modal feature exchange, saliency masking, or hierarchical
fusion \cite{zhao2025cmifdf,gao2026coifsd,an2026mrtdetr,zhang2026hffn,xiang2025unifusod,yue2022lowlight}.
Joint optimisation aligns the fused representation with the detector, but the
learned coupling is specific to a training objective. It does not itself
provide a label-free rule for choosing among already generated fusion
candidates when the target checkpoint or data composition changes.

\subsection{Evaluation and selection boundary}

Fusion quality has commonly been assessed with reference or no-reference image
metrics \cite{ma2019survey,mudavath2026review}. Those scores summarise source
retention or perceptual quality; they do not identify which of several inputs
will produce the higher AP for a particular detector. Multispectral
detection studies show that thermal evidence can improve robustness under
poor illumination \cite{li2010thermal,guo2024prior}, but gains depend on
architecture, training data, and the operating regime
\cite{abdar2021uncertainty,gawlikowski2023uncertainty}. The same dependence appears
visible across the detector checkpoints examined here.

The decision to depart from a visible-image fallback resembles the classical
reject option \cite{chow1970reject}, but the object of the decision is an input
candidate rather than a class prediction. The threshold must therefore be
specified independently of test-set performance, fixed before confirmation, and
evaluated against both average gain and worst-case loss. A fused-image score or
a single benchmark pairing alone leaves that decision boundary untested.

\subsection{Positioning}

CS-ECF does not introduce another image-fusion operator. It treats an existing
fusion library as a set of alternative inputs and uses the target detector as a
label-free evaluator. Unlike task-aware networks, it requires neither a new
fusion model nor joint detector--fusion training. Unlike fixed image-quality
rankings, it measures detector utility directly while accounting for
checkpoints, scene or frame groups, and independent evaluators. The contribution
is therefore an arbitration policy and a risk-boundary evaluation, not a claim
that any single fused representation is universally superior.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation}

We denote a registered visible--infrared pair by \(x_v\) and \(x_i\), and the
target detector checkpoint by \(\mathcal{D}\). A candidate
\(c\in\mathcal{C}\) produces an input image \(x_c\), where
\(\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{vis},\mathrm{cdf},\mathrm{pia},\mathrm{spg}\}\).
The policy returns one candidate per pair without access to ground-truth boxes:
\begin{equation}
g(x_v,x_i;\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{C}.
\end{equation}
The final detection output is
\(\mathcal{D}(x_{g(x_v,x_i;\mathcal{D})})\). Because the same detector is used
to evaluate candidates and to produce final detections, the policy requires no
separate label oracle.

\subsection{Fusion candidates}

The visible input is always retained as a safety candidate. CDF combines a
pixel-mean image and an IFCNN image using sign-consistent high-frequency
details. We denote the pixel-mean and IFCNN images by \(x_m\) and \(x_f\),
respectively, and let \(G_\sigma\) denote Gaussian blur with \(\sigma=5\). The
corresponding detail images are
\(d_m=x_m-G_\sigma*x_m\) and \(d_i=x_f-G_\sigma*x_f\). Their consensus detail
is
\begin{equation}
d(x)=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{sign}(d_m+d_i)\min(|d_m|,|d_i|), & d_m d_i>0,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A thermal-validity mask \(M\) combines the infrared top-hat response, infrared
intensity, visible luminance, connected components, and spatial smoothing. The
quantile \(q_{\mathrm{TH}}=0.95\) marks the top-hat response above which a pixel
is considered thermally salient. This quantity differs from the candidate score
\(q_c\) defined below. We applied a \(31\times31\) elliptical top-hat kernel and
an infrared floor of 120. Candidate regions also required a visible-luminance
cutoff at the 75th percentile, \(5\times5\) morphological opening, and connected
components of at least 64 pixels. The surviving mask was then dilated and
smoothed with \(31\times31\) kernels. Shared detail was injected into the LAB
luminance channel as
\begin{equation}
L' = L + \alpha M d.
\end{equation}
The frozen settings are \(q_{\mathrm{TH}}=0.95\), \(\alpha=0.20\), and
\(\sigma=5\). PIAFusion and SPGFusion were generated using their official model
definitions and pretrained weights under the same image-pair protocol
\cite{zhang2020,tang2022,zhang2026}.

\subsection{Confidence-shielded arbitration}

For candidate \(c\), let \(s_{c,1}\ge s_{c,2}\ge\cdots\) denote detector
confidences sorted in descending order. We define the candidate score as
\begin{equation}
q_c=\frac{1}{\min(K,n_c)}
\sum_{j=1}^{\min(K,n_c)}s_{c,j},
\qquad K=5,
\end{equation}
where \(n_c\) is the number of detections. Candidates with no detections receive
\(q_c=-1\). The highest-scoring candidate is
\(c^\ast=\arg\max_c q_c\), with ties broken in favour of visible. The selected
input is
\begin{equation}
c=
\begin{cases}
c^\ast, & c^\ast\ne\mathrm{vis}\ \text{and}\ q_{c^\ast}\ge1.5q_{\mathrm{vis}},\\
\mathrm{vis}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The complete inference path, including visible fallback, is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}. We fixed the four-candidate library and the top-five
score before confirmatory testing. The top-five mean reduces reliance on a
single detection while excluding the low-confidence tail. The candidate set
includes the visible fallback and representative region-selected, learned, and
published fusion methods. Before testing, we evaluated seven
margins from 1.0 to 2.0
\(\{1.00,1.10,1.20,1.30,1.50,1.75,2.00\}\) on 150 LLVIP validation images and
400 M3FD validation images, using two detectors per dataset. Our objective was
to maximize the dataset-balanced validation gain while ensuring that no
evaluator lost more than \(0.0005\) AP50. Under the fixed top-five rule, 1.5 was
the first margin to satisfy this constraint and produced the largest balanced
validation gain. At this margin, \(4.7\%\) of validation decisions switched to
a fused input, compared with \(11.4\%\) at 1.2. Table~\ref{tab:score-margin}
reports a post-freeze sensitivity analysis over other score depths and margins.
The expanded grid was descriptive and was not used to reselect the policy after
test results were observed. The complete pre-freeze search appears in the
Supplementary Material and is summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}.

\subsection{Datasets and evaluators}

The official LLVIP partitions are larger than the subsets used here
\cite{jia2021}. We uniformly shuffled the official test pairs with non-empty
labels using seed 0 and used the first 300 as the test subset. The release
contains 3,463 test pairs with non-empty labels, so 3,163 eligible pairs were
not evaluated. From the official training partition, we selected the
lexicographically first 1,500 pairs with non-empty labels as an experiment
pool. We assigned the first 1,350 pairs to model training and the remaining 150
pairs to validation. No image stem occurs across train, validation, and test
partitions. The three partitions contain 4,479, 419, and 744 pedestrian boxes,
respectively.

The eight LLVIP confirmatory checkpoints comprised visible-trained YOLO11n, a
modality-agnostic YOLO11n control, YOLO26n seeds 0--2, RT-DETR-l, and YOLOv8n
seeds 0 and 1. We used YOLO11n seed 0 for each YOLO11n family and retained four
independently trained runs per family for seed sensitivity analysis. All best
checkpoints were selected on the 150 validation images, not on the test subset.

M3FD uses a grouped six-class split containing 1,600 training, 400 validation,
and 200 test images \cite{liu2022}. We assigned frames to consecutive 100-frame
groups with a fixed group seed, preventing adjacent frames from crossing splits.
The released manifest specifies the two test groups. We selected one YOLO26n and
one YOLOv8n visible-trained six-class evaluator on the validation groups.

We evaluated 300 DroneVehicle image pairs from three fixed consecutive 100-frame
groups of the official test archive \cite{sun2022}. The groups were selected
with a predefined seed of 2037 before evaluation. The YOLO11x-OBB best and last
checkpoints come from one 150-epoch training run (batch size 32, 640-pixel input,
seed 0). Because these checkpoints share training data, we treat them as one
training-run cluster rather than independent architectures. The source archive
has 4,430,343,899 bytes and SHA-256
\texttt{\seqsplit{94BF47E493E7A57FD5D900439F614F339E7B6C7181E4D781BCFEBAEB963FFD8E}}
to identify the exact release used for the test groups.

\paragraph{External evaluator stress tests}
We also applied the frozen selector to three checkpoints that were not used to
construct the policy or select its margin. YOLOv9t and YOLOv10s were
COCO-pretrained models without LLVIP fine-tuning, and only their person-class
detections were retained \cite{wang2024yolov9,wang2024yolov10}. A third
YOLOv9t was initialized from the same COCO weights and fine-tuned for a
prespecified 40 epochs on the 1,350 LLVIP visible training images. Training
used AdamW, a learning rate of 0.002 with three-epoch warm-up and cosine decay,
weight decay \(5\times10^{-4}\), batch size 16, 640-pixel inputs, horizontal
flipping, and brightness/contrast jitter. We used a reproducible PIL-based
loader and the Ultralytics detection loss. We used the checkpoint from epoch 40
as the final model and did not use test results for epoch selection.

\paragraph{Detection evaluation}
Predictions use a 640-pixel inference size and a confidence floor of \(0.001\).
For YOLO models, non-maximum suppression uses an IoU threshold of \(0.7\). At
each class and each IoU threshold from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, predicted
boxes are assigned to ground-truth boxes by one-to-one maximum-cardinality
matching over valid pairs. The resulting true-positive flags and confidence
scores are pooled across images within a checkpoint. We integrate the resulting
precision-recall curve over 101 points using Ultralytics 8.4.189
\cite{jocher2026ultralytics}. AP50 is averaged over the classes present in the
dataset, and AP50--95 additionally averages the ten IoU thresholds.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Point estimates use pooled AP50 after concatenating detections across test
images within a checkpoint. Image-bootstrap intervals resample images within a
Bootstrap intervals for scenes or consecutive frame groups are the primary
clustered intervals. A detector checkpoint is the unit for architecture-level
summaries. Images and groups are
repeated observations within a checkpoint and are never treated as independent
training runs. Checkpoints from one training run remain one training-run
cluster. The dataset-balanced estimand averages within each dataset and then
weights LLVIP, M3FD, and DroneVehicle equally. Because M3FD contains only two
test groups and LLVIP contains seven scenes, the corresponding group intervals
are finite-cluster summaries.

The confirmatory multiplicity family contains the 12 prespecified
CS-ECF-minus-visible checkpoint contrasts. For each checkpoint, we used 2,000
paired scene- or frame-group bootstrap resamples to compute two-sided
bootstrap p-values. We then applied Holm correction at family-wise
\(\alpha=0.05\) and report Benjamini--Hochberg q values. We also summarise the
number of positive checkpoints with an exact sign test, which we do not treat
as a substitute for effect sizes. The dataset-balanced estimate uses 10,000
training-run-cluster-by-group resamples, and 20 independent 5,000-draw reruns
were used to check interval stability. The dataset-balanced contrast between
CS-ECF and Always CDF is a prespecified secondary estimand. Its per-dataset
values are descriptive rather than a separate confirmatory family. The three
external-evaluator contrasts form an exploratory stress-test family, and
Benjamini--Hochberg correction is applied across these checks.

We define detection-level ECE using ten fixed confidence bins and also report
the Brier score for the external evaluators. These quantities summarise
calibration for matched detections; they are not calibrated class
probabilities. Candidate selection uses the top-five confidence mean without
calibration adjustment.

The training-run-by-group bootstrap used the parameters above with
\texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng(2219)} in Python 3.10.22 and NumPy 2.2.6.
Spearman association tests used SciPy 1.15.3.
The Supplementary Material provides the complete split counts, all
per-checkpoint CS-ECF and Always CDF results, the validation-policy record, and
the multiplicity, baseline, class, calibration, and thermal analyses. The
public analysis script reconstructs each checkpoint
and rejects it if its AP does not agree with the published aggregate table
before computing the paired interval.

\subsection{Implementation and cost}

Using \(384\times512\) fusion inputs, FP32 precision, and a batch size of one on
an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti, the CDF pipeline required \SI{22.3}{ms}, PIAFusion
\SI{37.7}{ms}, and SPGFusion \SI{516.3}{ms}. Four serial YOLO11x-OBB forward
passes at 640-pixel input required \SI{130.0}{ms}, giving an estimated serial
total of \SI{706.3}{ms}. This estimate excludes model loading or swapping, data
loading, and final detection post-processing. The additional peak activation
memory of SPGFusion was \SI{1453.5}{MiB}; per-stage memory values should not be
summed as an end-to-end peak.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Fusion utility is evaluator-dependent}

Across the 12 confirmatory checkpoints, the same candidate set produced both
positive and negative AP50 changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:evaluator}). Nine of the 12
CS-ECF checkpoints had positive point estimates, and the dataset-balanced AP50
gain was 0.0133 with a training-run-by-group 95\% interval of
[0.0026, 0.0261]. The exact sign test for 9/12 positive contrasts gave
\(p=0.073\). After Holm correction across the 12-checkpoint family, no
individual contrast remained significant at 0.05. The smallest raw p-value,
for LLVIP/YOLO11n, was 0.005 (Holm-adjusted \(p=0.060\)). We therefore
interpret the balanced estimate as a small average effect and do not claim
checkpoint-specific significance (Fig.~\ref{fig:multiplicity}). The no-shield

\subsection{Controls and headroom}

To determine whether detector confidence adds information beyond image
quality, we evaluated label-free controls and a label-using upper bound. The
VIF selector chose the fused candidate with the highest mean reference VIF
relative to the visible and infrared inputs, and the SSIM selector used the
analogous mean structural similarity. VIF and SSIM selected only among the
three fused candidates, whereas entropy selection considered all four inputs.
All image statistics were computed on \(256\times256\) grayscale inputs, which
made the measures comparable across source resolutions and detector
architectures. At each checkpoint, we generated 500 uniform random candidate
allocations across images. The oracle
used test labels to choose the candidate with the highest per-image AP50 and is
an upper bound only. Images without ground-truth boxes received an AP50 of zero
for every candidate, so ties resolved to visible.

The maximum-reference-VIF selector achieved a dataset-balanced gain of 0.0008
and 8/12 positive checkpoints, well below the confidence-based policy. SSIM and
entropy selection produced strongly negative balanced gains. CS-ECF captured
about 40\% of the headroom between the mean uniform-random policy and the
per-image oracle on dataset-balanced AP50. Detector confidence therefore added
value beyond image-quality heuristics, although substantial headroom remained
(Fig.~\ref{fig:controls}).

\subsection{Fixed baselines and class concentration}

At fixed inputs, Always CDF had positive AP50 changes at 10 of 12 checkpoints and
a dataset-balanced gain of 0.0031, but remained negative on both M3FD detectors.
Always PIAFusion and Always SPGFusion had negative dataset-balanced gains
because their strong DroneVehicle performance did not transfer to the other
datasets. In the M3FD six-class evaluation, the CS-ECF gain was concentrated in
the people class: YOLO26n improved by 0.0214 and YOLOv8n improved by 0.1090.
The other five classes changed by at most 0.0030 in absolute AP50
(Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal}). The people class accounts for only 166 of 2,022 test
annotations (8.2\%), but AP50 weights classes equally. It contributed 87.7\% and
99.6\% of the positive class-mean gain for the two detectors, respectively.
Image-bootstrap intervals were \([-0.0070,+0.0498]\) for YOLO26n and
\([+0.0440,+0.1913]\) for YOLOv8n; only the latter excluded zero. With only two
test frame groups, these class intervals are descriptive rather than stable
group-level inference. The evidence therefore supports a people-specific effect,
not uniform six-class improvement.

\subsection{Thermal feature association and mask ablation}

On LLVIP, infrared top-hat contrast showed a weak positive association with
fused-input selection (Spearman \(\rho=0.151\), \(p=0.009\)), whereas visible
luminance showed a weak negative association (\(\rho=-0.104\), \(p=0.071\)). On
M3FD, thermal contrast showed a moderate positive association
(\(\rho=0.320\), \(p<10^{-5}\)). On DroneVehicle, both thermal contrast
(\(\rho=-0.403\)) and visible luminance (\(\rho=-0.441\)) were negatively
associated with selection. A high-thermal/low-visible median split did not
produce a systematically larger AP gain than the remaining images.

A fixed-input ablation showed a small but consistent thermal-mask effect on
DroneVehicle. The CDF gain was 0.0060 with the thermal-validity mask and 0.0057
with global detail injection for the best checkpoint. The corresponding values
were 0.0050 and 0.0038 for the last checkpoint. On average, the thermal mask
added 0.0007 AP50 relative to global injection.

\subsection{Registration sensitivity}

We estimated translation residuals for all 300 LLVIP test pairs using ECC on
normalized Sobel edges. The median residual was 2.53 pixels. The median
annotated person box was 109 pixels wide and 246 pixels high, so the median
residual corresponded to 2.3\% of the median box width and 1.0\% of its height.
On the 75 best-aligned pairs (residual at most 1.61 pixels), the mean CS-ECF
gain across the eight LLVIP checkpoints was 0.0007, with five positive
checkpoints. On the 75 worst-aligned pairs, the mean gain was 0.0020, with six
positive checkpoints. Most checkpoint-specific intervals still crossed zero, so
these subsets do not identify a distinct registration regime. They do show that
the observed gain was not confined to the best-aligned quartile. The same
residual audit was not available for M3FD or DroneVehicle.

\section{Discussion}

The central finding is that fusion utility depends on the evaluator and is not
an intrinsic property of a fused image. One candidate can improve a detector's
confidence ordering while harming another's, and ignoring this dependence can
lead to inputs that look confident but reduce AP. CS-ECF addresses this problem
by using the target detector as an arbiter and requiring a large
visible-relative margin before switching.

The shield's value is clearest in the worst case. On DroneVehicle, the
no-shield selector achieved a higher mean gain where PIAFusion and SPGFusion
were strong, but it also caused severe LLVIP losses. The 1.5 margin favours
visible fallback under ambiguous evidence. This behaviour is consistent with a
deployment setting in which a rare large regression is more costly than a
missed moderate gain.

The comparison with Always CDF further narrows the use case. When CDF is stable
for a fixed evaluator family, the simpler fixed input remains preferable.
CS-ECF is more relevant when the evaluator or data domain can change which
fusion family performs best, as observed across pedestrian, mixed-class, and
vehicle-focused evaluation. Its modest balanced advantage should therefore not
be read as evidence that it can replace CDF everywhere, because the interval
included zero.

The multiplicity analysis further limits the strength of the evidence.
Although the dataset-balanced interval excludes zero, no individual checkpoint
contrast survived Holm correction, and the 9/12 sign test was not significant.
These results support a small average effect under a prespecified composite
estimand, not checkpoint-specific improvement.

The external evaluators provide a more heterogeneous picture. Both zero-shot
checkpoints support a visible-relative gain but not superiority over Always
CDF. The LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t reverses the visible-relative contrast, while
shielding still improves on the no-shield selector. This checkpoint also had
higher calibration error and a higher degradation rate among switched images.
These observations are consistent with a calibration-related failure, but its
lower test performance and larger train-to-test shift provide alternative
explanations. The current design cannot separate these explanations.

Three experimental boundaries further constrain the interpretation. First, the
strongest M3FD effect is class-specific: people detection improves substantially
while the other classes remain nearly unchanged. Second, the DroneVehicle
confirmation uses two checkpoints from one training run, so the checkpoints are
correlated. Third, a target detector not included in confirmatory testing can reverse
the sign of the visible-relative contrast, as observed for the
LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t. The policy should therefore be validated for each
deployed evaluator rather than assumed to be portable.

A further boundary concerns the LLVIP training subset. It was selected
lexicographically rather than by scene-stratified sampling, so its scene
composition may differ from that of the full official training partition. The
test subset was sampled randomly from all 3,463 non-empty official test pairs.

The thermal analysis adds another limitation. Thermal contrast may increase the
likelihood of selecting fused evidence on LLVIP and M3FD, but the association
reverses on DroneVehicle. Sensor-level thermal evidence and detector confidence
therefore capture different information, and neither is a universal utility
score. The appropriate claim is detector-conditioned thermal fusion utility,
not thermal dominance.

Compute cost is also substantial. SPGFusion dominates serial latency, and four
detector passes add further cost. CS-ECF is therefore suited to settings in
which the target detector is already run and a large input-selection regression
is costly. It is not a low-latency replacement for a single fusion operator.
Candidate pruning and early termination remain untested; their latency-accuracy
trade-off requires a separate deployment study (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}).

A practical decision sequence follows from these boundaries:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item use Always CDF when the detector is fixed and validation supports stable
  CDF performance;
  \item consider CS-ECF when the evaluator or domain may change and the target
  model can be calibrated and revalidated on held-out groups;
  \item use the shield when a large regression is more costly than a missed
  moderate gain;
  \item avoid the four-candidate search for latency-sensitive deployment unless
  pruning or early termination is evaluated separately;
  \item revalidate the threshold after fine-tuning materially changes confidence
  calibration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

We showed that infrared--visible fusion utility depends on the evaluator and
proposed CS-ECF, a confidence-shielded arbitration policy with a fixed
visible-relative margin and explicit visible fallback. Across three datasets and
12 checkpoints, nine checkpoint point estimates were positive, but none
survived Holm correction and the exact sign test was not significant
(\(p=0.073\)). The dataset-balanced AP50 gain was 0.0133 with a 95\% interval
of [0.0026, 0.0261], while the magnitude of the worst observed loss decreased
from 0.0584 to 0.0040. Against Always CDF, the balanced difference was
\(+0.0102\), but its interval included zero, so dominance was not established.
Two zero-shot evaluators improved AP50 over visible by 0.021--0.025 without
separating from Always CDF. The LLVIP-fine-tuned YOLOv9t lost 0.0108 AP50
relative to visible, although the shield improved the no-shield selector by
0.0153. These results support bounded, evaluator-dependent risk control for
pedestrian-centric and mixed infrared--visible detection. They do not support
uniform six-class improvement, universal portability across evaluators,
independent replication of the DroneVehicle result from one training run, or
dominance over Always CDF. Deployment also requires consideration of SPGFusion
latency and the cost of four detector passes.
