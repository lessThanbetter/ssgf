Reviewer 1 - Initial submission, 16/10/2026
This is a measurement/benchmarking paper rather than a method paper. The authors
argue that the ranking of visible–infrared (VIS–IR) fusion rules is not a property of the
fused images but of the evaluator and of the construction of the fidelity index used to
score them. They build a targetness-weighted fusion family (a morphological top-hat
prior, a distilled learned probe of 0.07 M parameters, and an ensemble) plus activity-only,
permuted and weight-shuffled controls, compare against IFCNN, DenseFuse, PIAFusion
and SPGFusion, and then test fourteen fused variants against fourteen trained detectors
on two benchmarks (LLVIP, 300 test images; an aligned FLIR-ADAS release, 1500 train
/ 300 test). All detection contrasts are paired intervals resampled over the same images,
with multiplicities corrected. The paper reports five claims: (i) no fused input beats the
unfused visible source at mAP50 under a visible-trained detector, with deficits of 0.023–
0.152; (ii) the sign reverses under a modality-agnostic detector; (iii) the deficit is a
stratification (crowded, small-target, high-contrast) rather than a regime effect; (iv) task-
aware adaptation of the fusion network buys no net gain; and (v) the protocol, released
per-image scores, fused images and scripts, is itself the deliverable.
Major Comments
M1. The headline claim is contradicted by the table said to support it
Section 4 states that “Table 3 holds the fused images fixed and varies only the detector …
and no fused input reaches the unfused visible source under any of the three visible-
trained architectures.” Table 3 is printed “in the single-channel convention of the reported
protocol,” and in that convention the claim is false: under YOLO11n, top-hat fused
(0.8306) and ensemble fused (0.8291) exceed the visible source (0.8289); under
YOLO26n, activity-only (0.8310) and DenseFuse (0.8257) exceed the visible source
(0.8209); and under RT-DETR-l the visible source is 0.8624 while mean (0.8646),
activity-only (0.8836), probe (0.8651), ensemble (0.8708), DenseFuse (0.8715) and CSF
(0.8632) all exceed it. The claim is only true under the colour-scored convention (visible
source raised to 0.881), which the authors state is “the convention used above and in
Supplementary Sec. 13” but which is not shown in Table 3. The reader therefore cannot
verify the paper’s central result from the paper. This must be fixed by (a) printing Table 3
with the colour-scored visible source as an explicit column (or a second sub-table), so
that the headline contrast is visible in the manuscript, and (b) correcting the sentence that
describes Table 3, which as written describes a table that is not in the manuscript. I would
also ask the authors to state plainly, in the main text, that in the single-channel convention
used for all rows, the visible source is penalised and several fused inputs do beat it, since
this is the convention the paper defends as treating every row alike, and it is the
convention in which the negative result is weakest.
M2. The numbers in the text do not reconcile with the numbers in the tables
Several specific instances: - Section 4 states the visible source is “raised from 0.830 to
0.881 mAP50” by colour scoring, but Table 3 lists the visible source at 0.8289 under
YOLO11n, 0.8209 under YOLO26n and 0.8624 under RT-DETR-l. It is never stated
which architecture the 0.830 and 0.881 belong to, nor whether the same increment applies
to all three. - The deficits are quoted as “0.023 to 0.152 mAP50” throughout the abstract,
introduction and conclusion, but Table 3’s largest deficit is SPGFusion against the visible
source: 0.8289 − 0.7296 = 0.0993 under YOLO11n and 0.8209 − 0.7290 = 0.0919 under
YOLO26n. A deficit of 0.152 does not appear in Table 3 at all. The text later says
“SPGFusion by 0.091 against 0.152 under YOLO11n” (Section 5), which implies the

0.152 is the RT-DETR-l figure, but Table 3 gives 0.8624 − 0.8105 = 0.0519 there, not
0.152. Please state, for every headline number, exactly which table cell and which
convention produced it. - Section 5 states that under RT-DETR-l “the control row that is
mid-pack elsewhere becomes the best fused input of any kind under it once the reported
protocol’s single-channel convention is used for every row”, but Table 3 shows activity-
only at 0.8836 as the best under RT-DETR-l, and it is not clear which row is meant by
“the control row.” - The abstract says “all forty-two contrasts are resolved once the third
architecture is repeated” while Section 4 says the RT-DETR-l column in Table 3 “is one
training run; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Sec. 17 use the mean of three.” The reader cannot
tell whether the headline 42/42 result comes from the single run shown or the three-run
mean.
M3. The fidelity comparison is structured such that the authors’ own rules rank near the
bottom, which weakens the argument they are used to make
Table 1 shows the authors’ rules at VIF 0.3298 (probe) and 0.3561 (top-hat) on TNO,
against SPGFusion at 0.8135 and PIAFusion at 0.6051, that is, the authors’ rules rank
fifth and sixth of seven, and the learned probe does not exceed the published baselines
“on any benchmark” (Section 3). The paper’s central rhetorical move is that “the two
methods the fidelity index ranks highest are the two that lose most” (SPGFusion and
IFCNN). But since the authors’ own rules perform poorly on the very index being
criticised, the argument has an appearance of being self-serving: the index is denounced
because it favours methods other than the authors’. The paper would be considerably
stronger if it (a) stated explicitly that the authors’ rules also fail to beat the visible source
(top-hat −0.0504, probe −0.0552 against the colour-scored source, by the authors’ own
numbers), so that the negative result is symmetric and not an artifact of the authors’ rules
being favoured; and (b) reported the fidelity-versus-detection correlation separately for
the authors’ family and for the published family, so the reader can see that the mismatch
is not a property of one group. Note also that the manuscript never explains why the top-
hat prior improves VIF over mean fusion by 33–36% yet the learned probe, trained by
distillation against that same prior, is beaten by it on two of three benchmarks. That is an
unexpected internal result that deserves comment.
M4. The fidelity score reported for the authors’ own rules never appears in the detection
comparison, so the index-to-detection link is not shown for their family
Table 7 correlates fidelity with contrast “over the thirteen inputs for which both are
recorded,” but the reported-index column and the reference-VIF column are not
populated for all rows and the reference VIF is “rescored on the eight variants whose
fused outputs were retained.” The paper does not state which inputs are missing from the
correlation, nor whether the authors’ four rules (mean, top-hat, probe, ensemble) are
among the thirteen. Since those four are the only rules whose fidelity the paper controls,
the correlation that matters most for the paper’s thesis is the one over its own family, and
it is not separable from the table as printed. Please give the full fidelity-versus-contrast
data for all fourteen inputs in one table, with the missing entries explained.
M5. The stratification claim is not fully supported by the evidence presented in the
manuscript
Claim (iii) states that the deficit is “a stratification, not a regime effect,” resolved for all
fourteen rules in the crowded, small-target and high-contrast strata and for only two in the
bottom tercile. Figure 3a is said to count, “for each regime defined from the annotation
and the unfused visible source alone, how many of the fourteen inputs are resolved below
the source.” Three problems: (a) the strata are defined from the unfused visible source’s
own detection quality, which is the same quantity being compared, so the stratification is
defined partly in terms of the outcome and is not independent of it; (b) the manuscript
says the sign of the association between contrast and deficit “changes from sequence to
sequence” without quantifying how often, and sequence-level results are given only for
                                             3


the SPGFusion contrast (Table 11, restricted to sequences with at least twenty test
images); and (c) selecting the strata post hoc from the same 300 images used for the
headline claim, with no correction for the stratum search, means the “resolved for all
fourteen” statement carries an unquantified selection effect. Please report the strata
counts with multiplicity correction over strata, state whether a pre-registered stratum
definition would give the same partition, and give the per-sequence sign-count over all
sequences rather than for one contrast only.
M6. The paper’s own methodological argument is undermined by keeping its most
important evidence in an unavailable supplement
The manuscript repeatedly defers load-bearing evidence: the colour-corrected protocol
(Sec. 12, which is the convention that makes the headline claim true), the clean-split
protocol (Sec. 16), the per-seed tables (Table S7), the component ablation (Tables S1–
S3), the per-evaluator adaptation results (Tables S24, S25), the per-image scores and
fused images, and the “remaining metrics.” The supplementary file was not provided with
the review copy, and several of these items are essential to evaluating the central claims
(see M1, M2). For publication, at least the following must be in the main manuscript:
Table 3 in the colour-corrected convention (or both conventions side by side); the per-
seed values behind Table 6; and the exact definition of the two index constructions whose
disagreement is one of the paper’s two headline results.
M7. The paper does not explain what a practitioner should do, which is the stated purpose
The conclusion is that “a rule should therefore be reported with its evaluator and index.”
This is a statement about reporting, not about choice, and the paper explicitly declines to
recommend a rule. Given the empirical result that under a visible-trained detector nothing
beats not fusing at all, the actionable conclusion would seem to be that for visible-trained
detectors one should deploy the visible source directly, and that fusion is only justified for
detectors trained on thermal or fused modalities. The manuscript should state this
explicitly and discuss the practical consequence, rather than leaving the reader to infer it.
Relatedly, the paper should say what it recommends for the common deployment case
where the detector’s training modality is unknown or is itself part of the design decision.
M8. The modality-agnostic result needs a clearer account of what the detector was trained
on and why it is the right control
Section 4 and Table 3 report an “agnostic” detector under which ten of fourteen fused
inputs exceed the visible source (and the IR source reaches 0.9415, far above every fused
input). The manuscript states this detector was “retrained with the 300 test images held
out” but does not state what training data it was trained on, presumably both modalities,
nor how the fusion of the two sources into the training set was constructed, nor whether it
was given the same fourteen fused variants at training time or a different fusion rule.
Since the reversal of the sign depends entirely on this detector, its construction is a load-
bearing detail. Please specify the training modality composition, the fusion rule used to
build its training inputs (if any), and whether the “clean split” experiment of Fig. 4a uses
four independently trained runs of the same detector or of a different one.
M9. The “deficit is not uniform / one control is resolved above the source” result is
reported but never interpreted
The abstract, Section 4 and the conclusion all note that under RT-DETR-l “one control is
resolved above the source instead.” Which control, by how much, and why? A single
fused input that beats the visible source under a visible-trained detector, when the paper’s
headline is that none does, is a counterexample that the paper must confront rather than
mention in passing. If the counterexample is the activity-only control (0.8836 vs 0.8624
in Table 3 under RT-DETR-l, a +0.021 gain), that is a substantial and directly relevant
exception, and it happens to be the classical L1-norm activity weighting, i.e. the rule the
paper’s targetness machinery is supposed to improve upon. Please analyse this case in the
main text, and state whether the headline claim should be qualified to “no fused input
                                            4


beats the visible source except under RT-DETR-l where the activity-only control does.”
M10. The activity-only control outperforms the paper’s targetness rules under two of
three visible-trained detectors, which undercuts the framing
Table 3: activity-only reaches 0.8277 / 0.8310 / 0.8836 under YOLO11n / YOLO26n /
RT-DETR-l, versus top-hat at 0.8306 / 0.8170 / 0.8632 and probe at 0.8258 / 0.8128 /
0.8651. Under YOLO26n and RT-DETR-l the plain activity rule, which the paper defines
as the control separating targetness from activity, beats both targetness rules. Table 6 even
reports “probe − activity-only” as −0.017 under YOLO11n and −0.011 under YOLO26n,
i.e. the learned probe is worse than the parameter-free activity weighting. The paper
reports these numbers faithfully but does not draw the implication: the targetness
weighting that occupies Fig. 1 and Section 2 is, downstream, not the best member of its
own family. This should be stated and discussed, and the claim that the top-hat prior
“beats mean fusion” should be replaced by a statement about the full ranking within the
family under each evaluator.
M11. The () and (m) normalisation argument is asserted rather than demonstrated
Section 2 argues that the product (m), not (), sets how soft the rule is, “which is what
makes fidelity comparisons between rules with different weight spreads meaningful.”
Table 9 is said to report a temperature sweep with detection at four temperatures under
two evaluators. The claimed invariance of the comparison under the (m) normalisation
should be shown directly, e.g. by reporting the resulting weight distributions (mean and
spread of (w_v)) at each () and demonstrating that the normalised weights are comparable
across rules with different activity spreads. As written, a reader cannot check whether the
normalisation actually achieves what is claimed, and this matters because the paper’s
targetness rules differ from mean fusion precisely in weight spread.
M12. The single-channel convention, which the paper defends, is acknowledged to
penalise the visible source, but the paper then uses the colour convention for its headline
and the single-channel convention for its tables
The authors state in the limitations that “every input is scored in a single channel, which
we chose so that all rows are treated alike but which penalises the visible source, the only
input that could have been scored in the colour convention the detectors were trained on.”
This is a defensible choice for the table, but it makes the paper’s headline claim (which
requires the colour-scored source) and the paper’s table (which shows the single-channel
source) describe two different experiments. The honest resolution is to present both
conventions everywhere, and to state the sign of the result under each. The paper is
admirably explicit about this tension in the discussion, but the abstract, introduction and
conclusion quote only the colour convention while citing a table that shows the other.
M13. The second benchmark is reported as measured without colour rescoring, so the
replication does not carry the same convention as the primary result
The limitations state that the second benchmark (aligned FLIR-ADAS) “has no colour-
corrected rescoring, because its detectors were trained with a different budget and their
weights are no longer available, so it is reported as measured.” This means the replication
of the headline claim on the second benchmark is in the single-channel convention,
i.e. the convention in which the primary result is weakest. The replication therefore does
not replicate the headline result as stated. Please clarify in the main text which
convention Table 4 uses, and state whether the “no fused input beats the visible source”
statement holds on the second benchmark under the same convention as the first.
M14. The claim that “the penalty is set by the fused rule rather than by the task difficulty
or the detector family” is not fully supported
Section 8 asserts this, but the evidence shows the penalty varying materially with the
detector: SPGFusion loses 0.0993 under YOLO11n and 0.0919 under YOLO26n (Table
3) but only 0.0519 under RT-DETR-l, and the paper itself notes “the deficits under RT-
DETR-l are the mildest of the three architectures.” Table 6 shows the probe-against-mean
                                            5


contrast with disjoint four-seed ranges across the two YOLO families. The correct
statement is that the sign is set by the rule plus the evaluator’s training modality, while
the magnitude varies with the architecture by a factor of about two. Please revise this
sentence to match the data.
M15. The statistical framework is described as paired resampling but the family-wise
error control is not specified
The manuscript states that “every interval in this paper resamples the same 300 images
1000 times and applies one draw to both arms of a contrast” and that “the multiplicity of
the 42 headline contrasts is corrected.” The correction method is not named, and it is not
stated whether the correction is applied within each of the four evaluator blocks, within
each figure, or across the whole paper. Nor is it stated what the corrected criterion is.
Since the paper’s argument is explicitly a measurement-discipline argument, the
correction procedure must be stated precisely (method, family definition, and resulting
adjusted threshold), and the corrected intervals should be printed in the tables rather than
only in a supplement.
M16. The 0.023 lower bound of the deficit range is never checked against the seed spread
in the same table
Table 6 reports the four-seed ranges for the two YOLO families, e.g. probe − VIS at
[−0.043, −0.003] under YOLO11n and [−0.028, −0.008] under YOLO26n, and top-hat −
mean at [−0.002, +0.025]. The smallest headline deficit is stated as 0.023, but Table 6
shows that the seed range for at least one related contrast spans zero (probe − mean
+0.005 [−0.002, +0.014]; top-hat − mean +0.006 [−0.002, +0.025]). Please state, for the
specific rule that produces the 0.023 lower bound, its four-seed range, so the reader can
verify that the smallest headline deficit exceeds the seed noise. The paper is careful about
this for some contrasts and not for others.
M17. The segmentation experiment is barely reported
The manuscript states that the evaluation uses “two segmentation networks” and Table 14
includes an “mIoU” column under “the modality-agnostic segmenter” (mean 0.1225, top-
hat 0.1270, probe 0.1270, ensemble 0.1269). An mIoU of 0.12 is very low for a
segmentation metric, and the manuscript does not state what is being segmented, on
which dataset, with which network, or how this result relates to the paper’s argument.
Either report the segmentation analysis properly (network, dataset, classes, metric
definition) or remove it; as it stands it invites the reader to doubt the authors’
measurement care, which is the opposite of the paper’s intent.
M18. The manuscript never states the computational cost of the proposed rules in the
main text
Section 2 gives the probe at “0.07 M parameters and 9.4 ms per 512 × 384 pair,” and the
SI is said to contain the “inference cost.” Since one of the paper’s implicit arguments is
that the parameter-light targetness rules are a reasonable design point, the cost of all four
of the authors’ rules and of the four reproduced baselines should be tabulated in the main
text (parameters, latency, and memory), under the same measurement protocol. This
matters for the practitioner the paper addresses.
M19. The paper does not examine whether the negative result is an artifact of the
registration or the annotation
Both benchmarks are registered VIS–IR pairs with COCO-style person annotations. The
paper’s negative result could in principle partly reflect residual misregistration between
the modalities at the annotation level (the annotations are drawn on one modality and
used for both), or annotation conventions that favour visible appearance. The manuscript
mentions neither possibility. Given that the recommended conclusion is “do not fuse for a
visible-trained detector,” this alternative explanation should be ruled out, at least by
reporting the registration procedure and any alignment-error estimate, and by reporting
the contrast for the subset of images with the best alignment.

M20. Several internal inconsistencies, undefined terms and reporting defects
•The abstract says “fourteen fused variants, fourteen detectors and two benchmarks,” but
Table 3 lists twelve inputs (two sources plus ten fused rows including the four
permutations) and the text elsewhere refers to “the same twelve inputs.” Please reconcile
the counts.
•The keyword list under the title (“image fusion, infrared, visible, saliency, targetness”)
differs from the submission-system keywords (“infrared and visible image fusion, fidelity
metrics, evaluation protocol, object detection, multimodal pedestrian detection”); unify.
•Section 5 refers to “the eight variants whose fused outputs were retained” without stating
the retention criterion or why outputs were discarded from a study whose stated
deliverable is complete artifacts.
•Fig. 2a is described as “42 contrasts” (3 visible-trained architectures × 14 inputs) but the
abstract lists “fourteen fused variants”; 14 × 3 = 42 only if the two sources are excluded,
so the count of “fourteen” and the count of “42” are inconsistent. State the arithmetic.
•The manuscript refers to “our own rules” and “the reported index” without naming the
index until Section 3; the index should be named and defined at first use.
•Table 1, Table 2 and Table 14 report VIF for the same rules on the same datasets, but
Table 1’s top-hat TNO value (0.3561) and Table 2’s identical value appear to be the same
measurement reported twice with different comparison sets; make clear whether these are
the same numbers.
•The manuscript says “the two end-to-end networks dominate pixel fidelity” but does not
state that IFCNN (0.4440 on TNO) is beaten by SPGFusion (0.8135) and PIAFusion
(0.6051) but beats DenseFuse (0.3697) and both of the authors’ rules, the sentence should
name the networks.
•Figure captions and the algorithm listing are deferred to the supplement; at least Fig. 1’s
caption in the manuscript should fully specify the pipeline without requiring the
supplement.

Reviewer 2 - Initial submission, 25/10/2026
This manuscript investigates the relationship between evaluation metrics for infrared and
visible image fusion and downstream detection performance. It compares multiple fusion
methods, detectors, and datasets, and the topic is of potential research interest. However,
the following issues should be addressed:
 ．
1 The results in Fig. 5(b) appear inconsistent with the figure caption. The caption states
that none of the metrics maintains the same direction of correlation across different
evaluators. However, the correlation coefficients for SSIM\(_{\mathrm{vis}}\) are
positive for all four evaluators, as also reported in Table 7. Please check the caption and
describe more accurately the relationship between the fidelity metrics and downstream
performance.
 ．
2 For LLVIP, some detectors use the same 300 images evaluated in the study as their
validation set. Although the manuscript reports experiments with a partially revised data
split, this protocol has not been applied to all major evaluators and comparisons. Please
use results from a test set that is fully independent of the training and validation sets as
the primary results.
 ．
3 The authors note that the “reported VIF” and the reference implementation produce
opposite rankings because of differences in noise-parameter calibration. However, Table
1 presents the comparison simply as “VIF.” Please clearly distinguish between the two
implementations and report results recalculated with appropriately calibrated noise
parameters using the reference implementation for the complete set of methods.
 ．
4 The description of the detection evaluation procedure is insufficient. The manuscript
states that one-to-one maximum-cardinality matching is used and that AP is computed
using a detection library, but it does not clarify whether the matching is performed
according to the confidence scores of the predicted boxes or how the matching results are
used to calculate AP. Please provide these details.
